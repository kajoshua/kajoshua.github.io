# LESSONS LEARNED FROM SOFTWARE ENGINEERING

- A lot of software engineering involves creating development lifecycle process to protect complex systems against stupid mistakes by human developers.
- Being in industry realizes a weird dichotomy between "anyone can do X" and "I can do X." The former is valued in academia as intellectual novelty to the human race; the latter is valued as a professional skill to someone who needs that skill utilized. Although anyone can figure out X if it's known, you are in the priviledged position of being responsible for figuring it out.
- Understanding a software system thoroughly requires certain general background knowledge on a topic and specific knowledge of the design choices and trade-offs accumulated in the system. Documentation serves the epistemological purpose of explaining why certain choices are beneficial or too costly to optimize, for abitrary future developers.
- Software engineering is a time allocation optimization problem, where you trade-off importance and urgency with respect to fluctuating personal concentration throughout a day and personnel and resources.
- In large teams, you might have two subsets of groups who attend different meetings but don't communicate the findings to one another. Knowledge must be social, to some degree.
- Two key pillars are leverage (assets that you can hand off) and momentum (cognitive caching, processes in place that don't need re-creating, people who learn lessons over time)
- Good management tips:
  - incentivize behavior, not necessarily the outcome
  - Ask people what they think they should do in this situation
  - if people try experimenting, and risk is bounded, let them and see what happens
- The job of a software engineer is to structure ambiguity
- Automate any regular process taking up >3% of weekly time.
- Large organizations: culture, processes, repetitiveneess factor, hit by the bus factor, where do people go for information or to make suggestions, how well distributed teams can depend on each other, efficiency of CAPEX on each team, drive and initiative of individuals
- Companies are supposed to capture institutional memory. We shouldn't be learning how to do basic things on a project mid-way through, especially if others have learned the same lessons. make sure to write down lessons learned and what you hsould have done earlier in the project, had you realiezd it sooner, so that the next project doesn't have such a time delay. At least, if now ahat to do, you learn better the relative weights in priorities for tasks, by practice.
- Recall Diffusion of Responsibility: people need specific POC for certain tasks / scopes of responsibility, otherwise there lapses will forseeably occur
- Culture and norms affect cow trails, which incentivizes desired behavior, more so than laws outright.

# LESSONS LEARNED OVER LONG PERIODS OF TIME


Lessons you don't realize until you've worked at a firm for extended periods of time:
- If there's significant turn over, then not everyone knows everything. "I think it works like X" is commonplace. A well-functioning organization at least enables people to know who to contact for better information.
- Code debt you left yourself / are affected by decisions you made previously. It's different when you do it than when you adopt poor engineering from someone else.
- Learning to prioritize and execute (Jocko) in different time horizons.
- Working with peers with different experiences than you -- and how to resolve conflicts. If there was tension last week, and you're working together for 15+ weeks, you got to get over your hurt ego.
- If someone asks a question but it's different than you would have come up with a similar type of question, step into their shoes to see if it addresses the situation sufficiently or if you really need to override their perspective.
- Locked-in decisions: past a certain point, you can't structurally change your product anymore without incurring substantial opportunity cost.
- Stakeholders come in after the feature design and development phases, requesting changes
- Not defining scope of project or measures of success ahead of time.
- No company-wide norms to follow for how to run a successful data consulting project based on lessons learned from prior iterations
- Every production system should have enough documentation that a competent, well-meaning engineer who wasn't involved in it's creation can safely modify and operate it.
- Every user-specific choice built locks-in the supplier, since recreating all the features determined over a long period of time is costly.
- Trust is built over time. If I say I need surplus resources to do X, you trust my judgement to get it done without waste.
- Hundreds / thousands of decisions made over 6+ months that haven't been systematically logged and attributed. 
- You get to notice your own strength, weaknesses, and error rate over time, and which processes to put in place to manage your own personalized workflow
- Sometimes it just takes time to grow. The things you find important now / think about intuitively differ with experiences

- Watch yourself in different environments. When do you light up? How well can you concentrate / for how long before you get distracted? What distracts you -- food, caffeine drop, heat, noise? Your body fluctuates relative to a fixed environment: a school bell feels different to high school students depending on the time of day. How in-depth into a problem do you need to be in order to solve it correctly? What types of errors do you appear to make and can you build protective systems around them? Know your strengths and weaknesses, both professionally and personally.
