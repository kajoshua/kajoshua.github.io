Lessons from Long-Term Work on Software Projects

The following are several lessons that have become apparent to me only after working on projects and within organizations for extended periods of time. A common thread connects them: time creates context, but organizations do not automatically retain that context. Many of the things that become obvious after six months or two years are difficult to see when joining a project for the first time. If that knowledge is not transferred to other people or captured in the organization's information systems, it can disappear when people leave or change roles.

1. No One Knows Everything: Build an Information System

If there is turnover, or if a project is complex enough, then no one knows everything. So "I think it works like X" becomes a commonplace answer to an inquiry.

This is not necessarily a failure of an individual employee. It is the team's responsibility to create an information system that is accessible and traversable as needed. This includes soft connections like peer-to-peer networks, scratch notes like Confluence pages, and formal technical documentation. Each serves a different purpose.

The product or service should also be intuitive enough that where someone thinks the answer should lie, based on the context and framing of the product or project, is where the answer to that question actually exists.

This is one reason documentation is more than record-keeping. It is part of the infrastructure through which an organization retains and transfers knowledge.

2. Experience Changes How You Think About Technical Decisions

One of the differences between junior and senior engineering is the time horizon over which a decision is evaluated.

As a junior software engineer, you may primarily be thinking about how to solve the immediate problem. As a senior software engineer, you think about how the design choice today affects the system's complexity later on, in the event that you need to return to this decision.

This is one way code debt accumulates. A design decision that seems reasonable when made can create additional complexity for the next engineer who has to modify the system. Sometimes taking on that debt is the right decision, particularly when there are legitimate short-term constraints. The important part is recognizing that the decision has consequences beyond the immediate task.

The same principle applies to seemingly small coding practices. For example, duplicating a piece of code with a slight modification may be an efficient way to satisfy the immediate ask. However, this may violate longer-term engineering practices around maintainability, reproducibility, locatability and the ability to transfer knowledge, testability, and modularity for future adaptability and scalability.

Experienced engineers therefore learn to evaluate decisions not only by whether they solve today's problem, but by how those decisions affect the system when someone has to return to them later.

3. Seniority Also Means Managing Multiple Time Horizons

Similarly, senior engineers learn how to prioritize and execute based on priorities in the short, medium, and long term.

A short-term optimization can be perfectly reasonable while still being harmful to the project if it repeatedly accumulates costs that become visible later. The challenge is not to optimize exclusively for the future, but to recognize which future costs are worth accepting in order to satisfy today's requirements.

This is why engineering practices such as maintainability, reproducibility, locatability, knowledge transfer, testability, modularity, adaptability, and scalability matter. They preserve the ability of the next engineer to understand the system, modify it, test it, and make a different decision when the circumstances change.

In this sense, engineering quality is partly about preserving future options. A decision that makes today's implementation easier but substantially constrains tomorrow's choices may not actually be the simplest solution.

4. Long-Term Collaboration Requires Recovering from Difficulties

The way you interact with your colleagues is different if you have only been working with them for two weeks, two months, or two years.

Working with the same people over a long period of time creates opportunities for tensions to arise from differences in approaches, personal or professional friction, or more serious disagreements. Some social problems might not arise until you have already been working together for six months or more. Other problems may exist for a long time without being resolved and only become a significant problem much later. This is one reason this type of knowledge requires long-term participation: you cannot necessarily understand the working relationship from observing it during a short period of time.

The important lesson is how to recover from these difficulties. Any tension that arises has to get sufficiently resolved in order to continue a professional and productive working environment. The goal is not necessarily to eliminate disagreement or make everyone interact in the same way. The goal is to maintain a positive working relationship even after something has gone wrong.

This also applies to differences in how people reason about a problem. If someone asks a question differently from how I would have come up with a similar type of question, I do not necessarily need to challenge their question by explaining how I would have raised it. I need to step into their shoes and determine whether the ideas they are discussing are actually the ones needed in the situation.

As a manager-in-training, this seems particularly important. The job is not to punish non-100% accuracy or enforce perfect reasoning at every moment. It is to promote behaviors in the ecosystem that are moving in the right direction and can adjust over time.

5. Projects Become Increasingly Constrained as They Mature

When you are working on a project for more than six months, you become subject to locked-in decisions. Past a certain point, each change should generally only modify a relatively small percentage of the existing project.

Projects are often built according to an approximate 80/20 pattern. It may be relatively easy to build the first 80 percent of a project, but it becomes increasingly difficult to determine which edits are actually correct for the last 20 percent. At the same time, that last 20 percent can provide a disproportionate amount of the project's value.

There is therefore a contrast between diminishing returns and increasing importance. The additional work becomes harder and produces less incremental progress, but the decisions being made can matter more because they determine whether the project actually accomplishes what it was intended to accomplish.

This creates a form of lock-in. It is certainly possible to change direction later, but previous decisions have already consumed engineering effort, budget, time, and opportunity cost. If a substantial portion of the project is changed late in the process, those previous decisions are not simply being replaced with a better idea. The organization is overriding work that was already paid for, evaluated, and incorporated into the existing system.

This is why early decisions matter so substantially, and why maintaining project objectives in the middle of a project is important. It is not that changing direction is irresponsible or impossible. It is that the cost of overriding a series of high-cost and thoroughly evaluated choices becomes increasingly large as the project progresses.

The cost is primarily sunk and opportunity cost: the budget has already been spent, the engineering time has already been consumed, and that effort could have been spent on something else. Changing direction may still be the correct decision, but the prior investment does not disappear simply because the decision has changed.

6. Long-Term Projects Reveal Problems That Were Invisible at the Beginning

The longer you work on a project, the more you learn about problems that were not apparent during its initial design. This is not necessarily a failure of the original plan. Some things simply cannot be known until the project has progressed far enough for them to become visible.

For example, stakeholders may develop a different understanding of which phase of work the project is actually in. A stakeholder who becomes involved after feature design and development may request changes that are individually reasonable but arrive at a point where incorporating them is substantially more difficult.

Similarly, an initial design may turn out not to be sufficiently robust against the range of system inputs and parameters it eventually encounters. What began as a design and implementation project may therefore require an additional phase focused on robustness.

You may also discover that the project did not initially capture the right metrics. A project can satisfy its stated top-level objective while failing to measure the more specific outcomes that actually determine whether the system is successful.

Eventually, the project also requires an engineering implementation system: prioritization, sprint planning, development, testing, deployment, and iteration. These practices can feel like obvious parts of software development after you have worked on enough projects, but they are themselves things that have to be learned.

One lesson from working on projects over a long period of time is therefore that the project itself changes what you know about how the project should be operated. Practices that seem unnecessary at the beginning may become necessary later as the system, team, and requirements become more complex.

7. The Organizational Problem

These observations point toward a broader issue. Much of what makes someone effective on a long-running project is not a single technical skill. It is the accumulation of context: knowing why decisions were made, which tradeoffs have already been considered, how the system behaves in unusual circumstances, how stakeholders think, how colleagues communicate, and which problems tend to emerge later in the project lifecycle.

That context is valuable precisely because it takes time to acquire.

The problem is that organizations often depend on employees to accumulate this knowledge without creating equally effective mechanisms for preserving it. Some of the knowledge can be documented. Some can be encoded in software architecture. Some can be transferred through mentoring and social networks. And some can only be acquired through experience.

The challenge for an organization is therefore not simply to "document everything." It is to determine which forms of knowledge need to be preserved, where they should live, and how future employees can recover the context they need without having to rediscover it themselves.

Long-term employees naturally become repositories of institutional knowledge. When they leave, an organization does not just lose labor capacity; it can also lose accumulated context. The extent to which that knowledge survives is ultimately a function of the organization's ability to transfer and retain it.
